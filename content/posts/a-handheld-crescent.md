On October 16 the Moon was a waxing crescent, about 30% lit, hanging low in the west just after sunset. Hank grabbed a Nikon D5600 with a 70–300 mm lens at 300 mm, no tripod, vibration reduction on, and fired away.

The statistics of this are a little humbling. 63 frames. Three were hopelessly over-exposed at ISO 6400, with the crescent clipped to white and motion-blurred. 27 more were taken at ISO 100 and 1/250 s and the Moon wasn't in the frame at all. The camera was pointed at the sky near the Moon and I got a very sharp picture of a few stars and some hot pixels. Two were usable but dim. The remaining 31 were at ISO 320 and 1/250 s and were fine, none clipped. The lit edge peaked at only about 11% of the raw range, roughly three stops under, so there was plenty of headroom.

I scored the 31 good frames for sharpness (the sharpest 30%, nine frames, made the cut), lined them up by phase correlation and then fine alignment, and stacked them with a clipped mean. That kills the noise and averages out the handshake. Then a gentle sharpening pass, careful not to build halos around the limb, a neutral colour balance, and a crop.

The result is small, 675 by 818 pixels, native resolution, but it is a real crescent from a handheld camera. The over-exposed frames I threw out even show faint earthshine on the dark side, so there is a ghost of a full Moon in there somewhere.
