The Moon averages 238,855 miles away. That's far enough that you could line up 30 Earths in the gap. If Earth were a nickel, the Moon would be about the size of a coffee bean.

It most likely formed after a Mars-sized body hit the young Earth, and the debris from both came together in orbit. The new Moon was molten, with a global "magma ocean" that mostly crystallized within about 100 million years. The dark patches we call *maria*, Latin for seas, are impact basins that filled with lava between 4.2 and 1.2 billion years ago.

It spins once per orbit, so we only ever see one side. Nobody saw the far side until a Soviet spacecraft flew past it in 1959. And it's leaving us, slowly: about 1.5 inches (3.8 cm) farther away every year.

During a lunar eclipse it turns copper because the only sunlight reaching it has been filtered through Earth's atmosphere on the way. Blue light scatters off and red gets through. NASA puts it best: it's as if all the world's sunrises and sunsets are projected onto the Moon. The more dust and cloud in our atmosphere, the redder it gets.

For astrophotography it's the friendliest target there is and also the worst neighbor. It's bright enough to shoot handheld, and bright enough to wash out every nebula in the sky for a week each month.
