The Bubble is a shell of gas in Cassiopeia, blown by the wind from one hot, massive star, SAO 20575 (also called BD+60°2522). William Herschel found it in 1787. The star is thought to have about 44 times the Sun's mass, and its wind has piled the surrounding gas into a bubble a few light-years across. The shell is brighter on one side because it's running into denser gas there, which is why the star sits off-center.

It sits next to the open cluster M52. They're in the same frame from our backyard, but not at the same distance: M52 is closer. The wider field also takes in the Lobster Claw (Sh2-157) and NGC 7538, a star-forming region at the edge.

At 400 mm with the RASA 8, the shell itself is small, about 100 pixels across. You can tell it's a bubble. You can't yet see the texture in the shell wall. That's why we'll come back with the EdgeHD 8, at 2032 mm native or 1422 mm with its 0.7× reducer.

Our current best look at the Bubble itself is the November 14 stack: **230 of 234 × 30 s**, **1 h 55 m**, Celestron RASA 8 + ASI2600MC Pro + Celestron Ha/Hβ/OIII, calibrated with the November 15 flats, taken after the dew shield was seated under the rail. Color is from spectrophotometric color calibration (SPCC), measured for the three passbands of the Celestron 93619 filter: hydrogen-alpha at 656.3 nm, oxygen III at 500.7 nm and hydrogen-beta at 486.1 nm. The Bubble is mostly hydrogen, so mostly red.

The night before, November 13, we shot the same field with the shield still crooked: **207 of 212 × 30 s**, **1 h 43 m 30 s**. That frame is wider, and it's the one that shows M52, the Lobster Claw, and NGC 7538 in one view, presented rotated 180°. It stays here as the field shot.
