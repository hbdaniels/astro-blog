M31, the Andromeda Galaxy

CatalogM31, NGC 224
TypeSpiral galaxy
ConstellationAndromeda
DistanceAbout 2.5 million light-years
Apparent sizeAbout six full Moons across (roughly 3°) in long exposures
Magnitude3.1 (naked-eye)
Best seasonAutumn; highest in the evening around November

Andromeda is the nearest major galaxy to our own, about 2.5 million light-years away. The light in Hank’s subs left it before there were any humans to catch it.

Nobody can be credited with discovering it, because it’s been visible to the naked eye forever. The first written record is in the Persian astronomer Abd al-Rahman al-Sufi’s Book of Fixed Stars, from the year 964.

For a long time it was the “Andromeda Nebula,” a cloud thought to be inside the Milky Way. In the 1920s Edwin Hubble found a Cepheid variable star in it, now called V1. Its pulsing gave a distance far beyond the edge of our galaxy, which settled it: the universe is full of galaxies, and Andromeda is one of them.

It’s big in the sky too. The faint outer disk stretches about six full Moons across, which is why it’s such a framing headache for a small-sensor setup. We just don’t see most of it by eye, because only the bright core stands out. Its companion M32 is probably the leftover core of a galaxy Andromeda collided with long ago.

And it’s coming our way. Hubble measurements published in 2012 show Andromeda and the Milky Way will collide head-on in about 4 billion years. The stars are so far apart that they’ll almost never hit each other, and the two galaxies will eventually merge into one big elliptical galaxy.
